# M7-L6 – Cross-system synthesis and research methods {#L6}

**Course area:** M7 – Master’s-oriented computer science

**Level:** Master’s orientation

**Prior knowledge:** No fixed prerequisites specified.

**Note:** Learning course – Computer Science \| Master’s core area F, lesson 6 of 6

**Guiding question:** How does one move from a technical idea to a **traceable, verifiable and responsible decision** when algorithms, databases, distributed systems, security, AI, operation and costs all play a role at the same time?

This lesson concludes the Master’s core area. It connects the scientific reasoning from [M7-L1](M7-L1.md), the limits of formal correctness from [M7-L2](M7-L2.md), distributed system models from [M7-L3](M7-L3.md), architectural and security trade-offs from [M7-L4](M7-L4.md) and AI evaluation from [M7-L5](M7-L5.md). M7-L6 is not a repetition of these five texts. Its subject is **the method of bringing different kinds of proof and knowledge together into a robust overall assessment**.

## The actual research problem is rarely purely technical {#S1}

A small program can run quickly on one's own computer and reliably perform its task. Once it becomes an online service, the questions change. Several people are now active simultaneously, a server may fail, inputs may be malicious, personal data require protection, queries become expensive and an AI module might produce plausible but incorrect answers.

One can answer each individual question in isolation and still build the wrong overall system. A particularly fast algorithm, for example, is of little use if a database transaction creates duplicate entries on retries. An excellently rated AI is of little use if its document access bypasses permissions. A highly available architecture may incur unaffordable costs.

**Cross-system synthesis** therefore means that results from several disciplinary perspectives are not simply added together but examined for **interactions, shared assumptions and conflicting objectives**. A particular improvement in quality may depend on a prerequisite that is not met elsewhere.

``` text
User problem and context
          |
          v
     verifiable goals
          |
          v
    several solution paths
       /  |    |    \
      v   v    v     v
 Algorithm Data Security Operations/AI
       \  |    |    /
          v
 Evidence + limits + interactions
          |
          v
 reasoned decision and reassessment
```

**When is it useful?** Especially when selecting an architecture, planning migrations, undertaking research and automating decisions with real consequences. **When is it excessive?** For a private one-off script, a few clear criteria often suffice. The core of the method – writing down the problem, assumptions and limitations – remains helpful there too.

## Research, development and evaluation are different activities {#S2}

**Development** creates a working artefact: software, a database structure or a procedure. **Evaluation** examines particular properties of that artefact. **Scientific research**, in addition, seeks methodologically justified findings relevant beyond the particular case.

Working software is therefore not yet a scientific discovery. Conversely, a scientifically important investigation may end with a negative result: a sophisticated new architecture offered no advantage over a simple solution under realistic conditions.

In **design science research**, purposeful artefacts are designed and evaluated to gain knowledge about a problem and possible solutions. Hevner and colleagues bring problem relevance, artefact design and evaluation together in a common framework \[Q1\]. This is an established research direction, but **not every ordinary software development activity automatically becomes research as a result**.

The distinction can be illustrated by three statements:

- **Development report:** 'We implemented a search function with a cache.'
- **Evaluation report:** 'In a specified test, the measured response time decreased under these conditions.'
- **Research contribution:** 'We investigated under which load and data conditions caching is worthwhile compared with an index, tested competing explanations and formulated the limited scope of the results.'

The third statement requires traceable questions, appropriate comparative measures and an open account of uncertainty.

## A precise research question avoids many apparent answers later {#S3}

'Is the cloud better?' is too general. Neither 'cloud' nor 'better' designates a single measurable property. A testable question might be:

> 'For a media service with 100,000 metadata records and a predominantly read-oriented workload, does an additional cache layer reduce the p95 response time compared with the same database without a cache, without serving outdated updates more frequently than agreed?'

**p95** denotes the **95th percentile**: approximately 95% of observed requests take no longer than this time. The question specifies the system, workload, alternative, primary measure and a constraint. The 100,000 records mentioned here are merely a **didactic assumption**, not a performance specification for a product.

This includes four different statements:

1. **Research question:** What difference does the cache layer make under these conditions?
2. **Hypothesis:** It reduces p95 latency in read-heavy operation.
3. **Measurement definition:** What counts as a request, how is measurement performed and which requests are excluded from consideration?
4. **Refutation criterion:** What would fail to support the claimed improvement, such as no effect, high variation or violation of freshness limits?

A good question is not scientific merely because it contains mathematical symbols. What matters is that a possible finding could actually change its assessment. This builds on the work on models and hypotheses in M7-L1.

## From problem to claim: A traceable chain of evidence {#S4}

A scientifically usable architectural decision has several stages:

``` text
Initial problem
  -> objective and exclusion conditions
  -> alternatives
  -> model / hypotheses
  -> proof or testing procedure
  -> results and uncertainty
  -> decision with scope
  -> observation in actual operation
```

In practice, the sequence need not be strictly linear. A test may show that the research question was formulated incorrectly. A data protection requirement can immediately exclude an alternative. It is important that later results can visibly alter earlier assumptions.

**Claim, evidence and conclusion** should remain separate:

| Statement | Possible evidence | What remains open |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 'This algorithm returns the correct result for valid input.' | Formal correctness argument under defined assumptions | Hardware errors, incorrect implementation, invalid inputs |
| 'This query runs faster in the test.' | Measurement series, experimental conditions, variation | Other workloads, data distributions, future versions |
| 'This storage system survives the failure of one node.' | Failure model, protocol proof, failure experiment | Common causes of failure, operator errors, loss of an entire site |
| 'The AI identifies suitable document passages.' | Independent test corpus and reference judgements | New document types, untested user groups, attacks |
| 'This solution is sensible overall.' | Combination of criteria, risks, costs and decision-makers | Changed requirements, normative priorities |

The word **'supported'** is always tied to the specific claim and the conditions of the evidence.

## Four forms of knowledge must not replace one another {#S5}

At least four kinds of knowledge are needed in computer science:

**Deductive proof:** A statement follows from model assumptions and logical rules. M7-L2 explains, for example, why a correctly formulated loop invariant can contribute to a justification. A proof holds **within its model**; it does not automatically prove that the implemented application fully matches that model.

**Empirical measurement:** Actual processes are observed. Measurements can show running time, error rates or energy consumption. Uncertainty arises, among other things, from samples, environmental changes and measuring instruments.

**Simulation or model calculation:** Parameters are explored in a simplified model. This supports predictions, but is not evidence that a real system behaves exactly in the same way.

**Normative assessment:** People determine which consequences are acceptable: how important is privacy compared with convenience? What cost framework is affordable? What residual risk may an organisation take? An algorithm can support such value decisions but cannot legitimise them alone.

A common fallacy is: 'The measured value is more precise, so the decision is automatically correct.' In reality, a **justified objective** is additionally required. Measuring the exact latency of impermissible data processing does not make that processing acceptable.

## Assumptions and dependencies as an explicit system model {#S6}

A system can be described via its components as a directed **dependency graph**. A web application, for instance, depends on a database, an identity service and file storage. A document-supported AI assistant adds a search index, a language model and access-permission control.

``` text
Browser
   |
   v
Web application ----> Identity verification
   |                          |
   v                          v
Application logic ----> Access rules
   |    \                     |
   v     v                    |
Database  Document retrieval  |
             |                |
             v                |
          Language model <----+
             |
             v
        Answer with source
```

The sketch is a **logical view**; it implies neither that every component is a separate server nor that the language model itself reliably enforces permissions. Access rules must be implemented in trusted application components and enforced before protected content is retrieved.

Three questions aid the analysis:

- Which **functional chain** does a user action actually require?
- Where are the **common causes of failure**? A shared power supply may affect several supposedly independent servers simultaneously.
- What **chain of trust** arises? Not every result from one component may be accepted unchecked as an instruction to another.

The value of an architecture lies not in the number of components but in the manageability of its actual dependencies.

## Research design: Comparability means controlled, not artificially perfect {#S7}

An **experimental design** (design of experiments, DoE) specifies how alternatives are investigated so that observed differences can be meaningfully interpreted. The NIST/SEMATECH handbook covers, among other topics, measurement processes, experimental design and the limits of statistical conclusions \[Q2\].

For a database comparison, data volume, hardware, indexing, network path and cache configuration should not change simultaneously. Otherwise the cause of a difference in running time cannot be identified. **Confounders** are influences that vary together with the change under investigation and may bias the result.

Important methods:

- **Controlled comparison:** As far as possible, vary only the property under investigation between alternatives.
- **Randomisation:** Assign orders or test cases by a random procedure so that time of day and order effects do not systematically favour one variant.
- **Repetition:** Carry out measurements multiple times to reveal variability.
- **Blocking:** Compare within similar groups, for example treating small and large files separately.
- **Predefined endpoints:** Determine the main criterion and exclusion rules, wherever possible, before looking at the results.

A single attractive measurement is **not** reliable evidence. Conversely, absolute laboratory purity is not always possible. A realistic test under documented uncertainty is often more valuable than a perfect but completely unrealistic microbenchmark.

## End-to-end measures rather than the most attractive individual figure {#S8}

The term **end-to-end** refers to the entire chain of effects from initiating a user action to obtaining a usable result. A fast search index is of little use if the subsequent permissions check, network transmission or AI response takes a long time.

Consider an explicitly **constructed** response-time example:

| Step | Variant A | Variant B |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Authentication | 18 ms | 18 ms |
| Database query | 75 ms | 26 ms |
| Application logic | 24 ms | 29 ms |
| Network and display | 43 ms | 43 ms |
| **Total for one hypothetical execution** | **160 ms** | **116 ms** |

The sums are arithmetically correct: $A=18+75+24+43=160\,\mathrm{ms}$; $B=18+26+29+43=116\,\mathrm{ms}$. **This addition describes exactly one modelled execution**, not the p95 latency of a real system. In particular, individual component percentiles must not simply be added to obtain an end-to-end percentile; this requires the distribution of complete user requests.

In addition, **error frequency**, **response correctness**, **data freshness**, **data protection violations**, **costs** and **maintenance effort** may be relevant. Good systems research optimises perceived service quality, not merely the most impressive component metric.

## Average, edge case and failure: Different system properties {#S9}

A service that is fast on average may be unusable for the slowest 1% of requests. Low mean latency does not answer whether p99 latency is critically high. Similarly, high average availability says little about the clustering of failures during an important time window.

Research on **tail latency**, the slow ends of running-time distributions, demonstrates the importance of such edge cases for distributed services; the underlying ideas are technically introduced in M7-L3 \[Q3\].

A **service level indicator (SLI)** is a measured indicator of service quality, such as the proportion of successful requests. A **service level objective (SLO)** is a target for that indicator. SRE practice (*site reliability engineering*) uses such targets to balance reliability and development effort in a reasoned way \[Q4\].

An example that is not an actual operator's promise: an SLO might require at least 99.5% of eligible user requests to succeed within a defined observation window. To test the statement, a precise definition of **eligible**, **successful**, the time window and measurement location is required. With few requests, proportions are particularly uncertain statistically.

**Failure tests** supplement performance measures: how does the application behave during a database restart, an aborted payment, a failed deployment or an unavailable AI service? A system should degrade in a traceable way as far as possible, rather than silently returning incorrect results.

## Interactions that isolated tests overlook {#S10}

Many technical decisions entail **interactions**. A cache improves read rates but may serve stale information. More replicas may improve availability but incur additional synchronisation and operational costs. An AI assistant saves time while increasing the need for source verification and permission boundaries.

Example of stepwise media analysis:

``` text
Upload file
  -> extract metadata
  -> store results
  -> generate AI description
  -> editorial review
  -> publish
```

If metadata extraction writes twice during a retry, the system may create duplicate entries. An AI might then refer to the wrong version. What was originally a local error becomes a problem of data integrity, domain quality and ultimately user trust.

**Idempotency** here means that repeating the same domain-level job produces no unwanted additional effects. It does not guarantee that a message is physically transmitted exactly once over the network. This fundamental principle connects M1-L4, M7-L3 and M7-L4.

During synthesis, **interfaces and state transitions** must therefore be tested, not merely individual modules. The transitions between responsible components are often precisely where apparently good partial results fail.

## Distinguishing objectives, minimum conditions and preferences {#S11}

Not every property may be traded for an arbitrary amount of speed or money. **Minimum conditions** are exclusion criteria. **Preferences** only subsequently help rank permissible alternatives.

An example:

| Criterion | Type | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Protected documents available only to authorised users | Minimum condition | A variant without reliable permission checking is excluded |
| Recovery tested in practice | Minimum condition, if business-critical | Require verifiable recovery before release |
| p95 response time | Preference with threshold | Lower is useful if it benefits the intended use |
| Monthly operating expenditure | Preference with budget limit | Costs must remain affordable |
| Modifiability and comprehensibility | Preference | Less coupling may simplify maintenance |

The threshold 'business-critical' must be concretely defined. A private collection of notes may be protected differently from a system that processes payments or sensitive health data.

A **Pareto-dominated** alternative is one that is no better in every dimension considered and worse in at least one than another alternative. Under the stated assumptions, such an alternative is hardly attractive. With **Pareto-non-dominated** alternatives a genuine conflict of objectives remains; additional priorities are needed here.

## Decision matrices: Useful, but not truth machines {#S12}

A weighted decision matrix makes priorities visible. However, arbitrary weights and subjective ratings may just as easily conceal a preconceived choice. The calculation below serves **only** as a learning example.

Assume two permissible architectures have the following ratings on a **constructed** scale from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong):

| Criterion | Weight | A: Simple single instance | B: Multi-service architecture |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Comprehensibility/maintenance | 40% | 5 | 2 |
| Operating costs | 30% | 5 | 2 |
| Fault tolerance | 20% | 2 | 4 |
| Independent scaling | 10% | 2 | 5 |
| **Weighted overall rating** | **100%** | **4.1** | **2.7** |

The calculation is $A=0{,}4\times5+0{,}3\times5+0{,}2\times2+0{,}1\times2=$ **4.1**. For B, $0{,}4\times2+0{,}3\times2+0{,}2\times4+0{,}1\times5=$ **2.7**. The matrix shows that **under these chosen weights**, A wins. It does **not** show that A is objectively the better architecture or automatically more secure.

A score is not a directly measured natural constant. Hard protection and legal requirements must be satisfied **beforehand** for selected alternatives. Figures for cost or latency should wherever possible be measured rather than freely rated; subjective criteria must be indicated as such.

## Sensitivity analysis: Would the decision reverse under different priorities? {#S13}

A **sensitivity analysis** changes assumptions and observes whether the conclusion remains stable. In the preceding example, A depends heavily on the high weight assigned to simple maintenance and costs. For a second, likewise **freely invented**, emphasis of requirements, change the weights to **10% maintenance, 10% operating costs, 45% fault tolerance and 35% independent scaling**. The ratings of both architectures remain unchanged. This gives:

- **A:** $0{,}10 \times  5 + 0{,}10 \times  5 + 0{,}45 \times  2 + 0{,}35 \times  2 = 2{,}60$.
- **B:** $0{,}10 \times  2 + 0{,}10 \times  2 + 0{,}45 \times  4 + 0{,}35 \times  5 = 3{,}95$.

Under the **new preferences**, the ranking reverses: B is ahead of A. This is genuine computational sensitivity, but still **not empirical evidence** for B's availability. If the requirements are additionally tightened so that only a demonstrably fault-tolerant multi-node architecture meets the minimum conditions, A is **no longer permissible** irrespective of its previous score. B must also first demonstrate the required properties.

Other useful questions are:

- Does the result change if demand is ten times as high?
- What if network traffic rather than computing time determines the costs?
- Which alternative remains usable if an external service fails?
- How strongly does the recommendation depend on unsubstantiated estimates?
- Does an apparent improvement turn out to be only marginal under actual load?

A decision is **robust** not because all assumptions are positive, but because it remains viable under plausible changes in its assumptions or has a known fallback path.

## Research and architectural decisions have different time horizons {#S14}

A benchmark compares systems in a particular state. An architecture, however, is maintained over months or years. New software versions, different data volumes and changes of personnel can alter its properties.

A distinction is therefore made between:

- **One-off costs:** Design, migration, checks and initial setup.
- **Recurring costs:** Computing, storage, data traffic, licences, backups, monitoring and maintenance.
- **Change costs:** How much effort will the next modification or a switch to another provider require?
- **Risk and outage costs:** What damage can arise from data loss, security vulnerabilities or prolonged unavailability?
- **Opportunity costs:** What other useful work is left undone because a complex architecture consumes considerable attention?

**Total cost of ownership (TCO)** considers total costs over a chosen period. A single monthly invoice from the cloud portal is not complete evidence for it. A central research aspect is added here to the M7-L4 discussion of architecture: **time and future change are part of the model assumptions**.

When future demand cannot be estimated, an initially simple, well-modularised architecture with a traceable growth path may be more sensible than immediate distribution across many services. Not every future possibility must already be paid for technically today.

## Security and data protection belong in the research design {#S15}

Technically capable systems can be unacceptable because of impermissible data processing or abusive access. Security and data protection questions should therefore be considered as early as the selection of data, the test setup and the presentation of results.

The NIST document **SP 800-160 Volume 1 Revision 1** treats security as part of systems engineering throughout the lifecycle \[Q5\]. Its contribution is methodological: protection is not regarded after the fact as an additional product property.

When comparing two search methods, this may mean:

1. Use synthetic or lawfully supplied data for measurements.
2. Do not unnecessarily write sensitive content into raw logs.
3. Model test users and permissions so that no concealed grants arise.
4. After completion, control data retention, deletion and access.
5. Do not attribute protection against personal inferences to the purported anonymisation of published datasets without verifying it.

**Security assessment** is more than a penetration test and **data protection assessment** more than encryption. Both concern purposes, roles, lifecycles, misbehaviour and the limits of permissible operation. This lesson is not binding legal advice.

## Distinguishing reproducibility, replicability and generalisability {#S16}

Three terms sound similar but are not used consistently. In the sense of the **National Academies** report \[Q6\]:

- **Computational reproducibility:** Consistent results can be obtained using the same data, methods, computational steps and relevant analysis conditions.
- **Replicability:** New investigations of the same scientific question obtain consistent results with their own data, taking uncertainty into account.
- **Generalisability (generalizability):** Results also hold in environments or populations other than those of the original investigation.

**Important:** Some computer science conferences or other disciplines use 'reproduced' and 'replicated' differently. For publications, the **specific definition** must therefore be read; identical words do not guarantee identical evaluation methods.

Instructions that rerun a benchmark improve reproducibility. An independent working group on different servers can contribute to replication. Whether a result also applies to a fundamentally different load or different users is an additional question. The three levels do not automatically entail one another.

## Research artefacts: What would another person need to receive? {#S17}

A **research artefact** is a verifiable component of an investigation, such as source code, a data schema, test data, a measurement script, a configuration file, a statistical analysis procedure or a formal specification. For a repeatable investigation, the following information should be traceable:

| Artefact component | Why important? |
| --- | --- |
| Specific software and library versions | Behaviour may change between versions |
| Data provenance, filters, cleaning rules | Selection decisions affect the result |
| Database model, indices, data size | Performance depends strongly on these details |
| Environment and load generator | Hardware and request distribution affect measurements |
| Measurement procedure and raw results | Aggregated values alone conceal outliers and errors |
| Analysis, exclusions and statistics | Figures must remain interpretable |
| Limitations, licences and access rights | Not everything may be made publicly available |

The **ACM** uses its own research artefact review procedures; these distinguish, for example, whether materials are available, usable or reusable, or whether results have been independently confirmed \[Q7\]. Such labels are **not a general security guarantee**.

For data, the **FAIR principle** is also useful: *Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable* \[Q8\]. **Accessible does not automatically mean public**: controlled, legally permissible access can also be an important element.

## Describe uncertainty systematically rather than concealing it {#S18}

Two results with the same score may have different degrees of reliability. A value from a hundred independent test cases must be assessed differently from a value from ten almost identical test cases. A measurement from a stable environment may offer better comparability than one from a highly fluctuating production system.

Relevant types of uncertainty include:

- **Random variation:** Repeated measurements fluctuate.
- **Systematic bias:** The testing procedure favours particular cases or alternatives.
- **Model uncertainty:** Important parts of reality are not fully represented.
- **Parameter uncertainty:** Load, failure frequency or usage patterns are only estimated.
- **Normative uncertainty:** Participants weight risks and interests differently.

A **confidence interval** is a range calculated under certain model assumptions using a procedure intended to cover the true parameter with a specified long-run frequency over repeated samples. It is **not** the probability that the fixed true value lies in this exact interval after observing these data. M7-L5 provides specific worked examples.

For an architectural decision, a simple **uncertainty log** may suffice: 'Latency measured; long-term costs estimated; failure behaviour modelled only; data protection controls not yet independently reviewed.' This wording is more informative than a single overall traffic-light indicator.

## Learning from negative results and stopping criteria {#S19}

A research approach has not failed merely because the original hypothesis is not confirmed. If a distributed database design requires more effort and is slower than a single instance under realistic requirements, that is a **valuable finding**, provided the comparison was fair.

Useful stopping criteria may include:

- The expected benefit is below a previously specified threshold of practical relevance.
- A hard security or data protection condition is not met.
- Costs exceed the affordable limit.
- The measured improvement disappears after accounting for variation.
- The proposed solution relies on assumptions that do not hold at the deployment site.

A negative result should not be concealed or reinterpreted through an ever-changing set of metrics. **Exploratory analyses** may suggest new hypotheses, but their results must be identified as retrospectively discovered. Robust confirmation requires new, suitable data or a dedicated independent investigation.

## Case study A: Media archive – search, database and user experience {#S20}

A local media catalogue is intended to grow. Initially a file suffices. Later, 500,000 entries must be searched quickly and updated concurrently by a few users. Three options are conceptually available:

**A:** structured files with custom search logic; **B:** a local relational database with suitable indices; **C:** a database server with a web application. These alternatives do not meet exactly the same operational requirements. Cloud infrastructure becomes relevant only if external online access is needed.

**Research question:** Which data storage approach provides adequate response times, traceable changes and acceptable maintenance effort for a defined search profile?

**Investigation method:** A common dataset, documented search queries, identical hardware for local variants, separate assessment of server operation and repeated measurements after plausible warm-up and modification phases. Alongside p50/p95, data integrity, correctness of search results and modifiability are assessed.

**Potential finding:** A local database might already meet the need. A public server would then not be a better 'database solution', but an appropriate operational option only upon the emergence of a new domain requirement. The result would not be that servers are bad, but that **requirements and architectural measures must correspond**.

**Connections:** M1-L3 and M10-L2 explain data structures and queries; M3-L1–M3-L6 data storage; M7-L1 the experimental question; M7-L4 the operational consequences. This is a **hypothetical teaching scenario with no benchmark conducted**.

## Case study B: Cloud service – reliability versus complexity {#S21}

A service accepts analysis jobs. A user uploads files; processing may take several minutes. A simple implementation handles every job directly within the web process. A more elaborate alternative decouples the web server, queue and workers.

``` text
Direct variant:
Browser -> Web server -> Computation -> Response

Decoupled variant:
Browser -> Web server -> Job database
                          |
                          v
                         Queue
                          |
                          v
                        Worker -> Results database
                          |
                          v
                       Status query
```

**Hypothesis:** The decoupled variant keeps short web requests responsive even when individual computations take a long time. **Counter-hypothesis:** The additional operational effort provides insufficient benefit for the current volume but creates new types of error.

**Method:** Vary arrival rates, computation duration and concurrent jobs; deliberately account for errors and retries; verify domain-level job success by unique identifier. Measurements include waiting time until the result, responsiveness when creating a job, duplicate side effects and recovery after worker failure.

**Limitation:** A queue does not make processing reliable by itself. It shifts the problem to persistence, retries, job status, dead-letter processing and responsibilities. Under very light load, the direct call may remain more suitable. This is the same lesson as in M7-L3: **distribution is a means, not a quality objective in itself**.

## Case study C: Document-supported AI assistant – quality and protection together {#S22}

An organisation wants to make internal documents queryable through an assistant. Possible approaches include conventional full-text search and search with **retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)**, in which retrieved document passages are supplied to a language model as context.

**Research questions:** Do users find the correct passage faster? Are the answers supported by evidence? Do documents beyond their permissions remain invisible? What types of error and operational costs arise?

**Evaluation design:** A representative set of real, lawfully selected questions is prepared with reference answers and associated document passages. Permission configurations are part of the test plan. For each alternative, retrieval accuracy, unsupported claims, citation quality, response time and the need for human corrections are evaluated separately.

A modelled process:

``` text
Question + verified identity
           |
           v
 Permission-checked document retrieval
           |
           +------> suitable source available?
                        |             |
                       yes            no
                        |             |
                        v             v
                   Model answer    “Not supported”
                        |
                        v
                 Citation + verification
```

The sketch is not a guarantee that a language model will independently refrain from answering when no source is available. The **application system** must control the handling of missing or prohibited sources. A prompt alone is not a reliable authorisation boundary.

**Possible conclusion:** A generative system may be useful for particular question types without fully replacing conventional search. Likewise, in a narrow environment the safer or less costly choice may be precisely the one **without** a language model. The evaluation procedure draws methodologically on M7-L5, the permissions and risk analysis on M7-L4. This scenario too has **not been tested in reality**.

## Synthesis example: A judgement with several kinds of evidence {#S23}

Suppose the preceding studies had actually produced results. A rigorous final account would need a structure such as the following; the rows are **templates for types of evidence**, not claimed research findings:

| Evaluation dimension | Required statement | Type of evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Functional correctness | Jobs do not take effect twice | Invariants, integration and retry tests |
| User-facing performance | p95 end-to-end latency is within the target range | Measurement series under a specified load profile |
| Reliability | Recovery after a defined failure succeeds | Failure test with observed result |
| Data protection | Unauthorised users do not see protected documents | Permissions model, negative access tests, review |
| AI quality | Answers have verifiable sources or acknowledge lack of knowledge | Independent test corpus, error taxonomy |
| Economic viability | Costs remain within the agreed framework | Model calculation plus actual cost observation |
| Maintainability | A change remains manageable through limited interfaces | Architecture review and documented change path |

An architecture should be approved only for the **demonstrated conditions**. If permissions checking was not investigated, this must remain explicitly open. It must not be treated as 'also demonstrated' by good AI retrieval rates.

## Responsibility is not another numerical measure {#S24}

Scientific quality and practical responsibility overlap but are not identical. A study may demonstrate with flawless methodology that certain users are served faster. Whether disadvantaging other users is acceptable requires additional normative and potentially legal assessment.

Responsibility for decisions should remain identifiable:

- **Domain responsibility:** What must the system achieve for its intended purpose?
- **Technical responsibility:** What guarantees and limits does the implementation have?
- **Operational responsibility:** Who notices outages, restores data and decides on changes?
- **Data protection and security responsibility:** Who reviews purposes, access and potential consequences for affected people?
- **Scientific responsibility:** Who separates claims, data, interpretation and conflicts of interest?

With **GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE and MANAGE**, the NIST framework for AI risks also emphasises responsibilities and continuing risk management \[Q9\]. A documented procedure is helpful but does not replace actual control and is not certification.

Especially for decisions that matter to people, a system must also offer a route for **corrections, objections and clarification of responsibility**. A high accuracy rate alone cannot compensate for its absence.

## The scientific report as a record of the decision {#S25}

A good final report is not as long as possible, but **traceable**. It distinguishes at least:

1. **Question and context:** What problem had to be solved? For whom and under which operational assumptions?
2. **Alternatives:** Which simple and complex solutions were seriously examined?
3. **Hard conditions:** What must not be violated irrespective of the score?
4. **Methods:** Which proofs, measurements, simulations and domain reviews were used?
5. **Results:** What was actually observed, with what variation and exceptions?
6. **Interpretation:** Which explanation is plausible, and which competing explanation remains possible?
7. **Decision:** Which alternative is preferred, and for which traceable reasons?
8. **Limitations and follow-up:** What is still untested, and when must the decision be reassessed?

An architectural decision can be recorded as an **architecture decision record (ADR)** – a short documented architectural decision. What matters is not the exact template but the separation of initial context, options, decision and consequences. If a new decision is made later, the previous state need not be made invisible: a traceable history protects against repeated mistakes.

The statement 'The available data are not yet sufficient for a decision' is also scientifically sound. An outstanding check is not a flaw as long as it is clearly identified and no unjustified certainty is suggested.

## Putting open research questions in context {#S26}

The end of a Master’s core course contains not only finished answers. Advanced computer science thrives on open questions such as:

- How can formal guarantees for a small critical component be meaningfully combined with empirical tests of a large overall system?
- How can the effects of AI assistants be assessed over long periods when users change their behaviour because of the system?
- How can a service make trustworthy decisions when data provenance, software components and conditions of use continually change?
- When does the improvement in availability from a distributed architecture justify the additional failure and operational surface?
- Which scientific statements remain possible if complete raw data cannot be published for data protection reasons?

These are **research questions**, not claims about problems already solved. Addressing each requires a suitable model and a verifiable method. Even for undecidable problems, restrictions, special cases and approximation procedures may be scientifically fruitful without overriding the impossibility result from M7-L2.

## Final thought: Good computer science is reasoned limitation {#S29}

The most important insight of the entire Master’s core area is not that more complex systems are fundamentally better. It is:

**A good technical decision is the simplest sufficiently viable solution whose assumptions, limitations, costs and potential harms have been understood and made verifiable.**

Sometimes that is a local program with a few files. Sometimes it is a relational database, a single server or a modular monolith. Other requirements call for replication, distributed protocols, elaborate security architecture or an AI system with structured human oversight.

Scientific work helps make the limits of a claim visible. Formal methods help rule out certain classes of error. Measurements show how a particular system behaves under particular conditions. Responsible architectural work adds the interests of affected people and the consequences throughout the lifecycle. **Only their reasoned combination produces a robust judgement.**
